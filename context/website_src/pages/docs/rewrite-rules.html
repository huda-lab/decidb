The nine rules

Each rule answers one class of problem with a known exact algorithm. The rule id (S1, A1, and so on) and the rule name are what EXPLAIN prints.

How a rule recognizes its class, builds its plan and hands back DECIDE's columns is on Query rewriting. This page covers what each rule answers. Every section ends with a runnable query; the site's build runs it and checks that EXPLAIN names the rule.

How the timings are measured

Every timing on this page comes from benchmark/decide/direct_solve/run.py on an 11-core Apple M3 Pro, over TPC-H tables (most over the 800,000 rows of partsupp at scale factor 1), in November 2026. Against Gurobi runs a fresh decidb process per run and takes the median of 3; the times include about 20 ms of process start and database open, and the two objectives agreed within 1e-6. Against a hand-written plan sums the CPU time of every operator, best of 5 in one process, beside a SQL query an expert wrote for the same answer with the same checks; a ratio of 1.0 means the rule's plan costs what that SQL costs. Performance describes the harness.

S1: choose rows by score

S1 answers "choose rows by score, with limits on how many": one BOOL per row, a count limit per group or over all rows, and a linear score.

Its class is the uniform matroid: the family of every set of at most k items. On a matroid, choosing items greedily by weight gives an optimum.

The shape. A query is S1 when it has:

  • one decision, a BOOL per row;
  • count limits on SUM(x) (<=, <, >=, >, =, or several forming one interval), all with the same by key and the same when. The body's constant coefficients must add up to exactly 1 or -1, so SUM(2*x - x) and SUM(-x) are counts;
  • limits that are constants below 1e20 in magnitude, values of the row, or aggregates of the group, such as SUM(x) <= 0.1 * COUNT(*) for the best tenth of the rows;
  • a linear objective SUM(score * x), maximized or minimized, or none;
  • other clauses only to fix single rows: x = 1, x <= flag, under a when or not.

The algorithm. For each group, with lower limit L and upper limit U:

  1. Score. Compute each row's score, oriented so that a positive score improves the objective.
  2. Rank. Number the rows by score, best first, with ROW_NUMBER over the group.
  3. Size. With p rows of positive score, take k = LEAST(U, GREATEST(L, p)) rows, less the rows fixed to 1.
  4. Choose. Set x = 1 on the rows ranked within k.

Why it is exact. For any fixed count m, the m best scores are optimal: swapping a chosen row for an unchosen one with a higher score cannot lower the total. The total grows while the next score is positive and shrinks once it is negative, so the best count with no limit is p, and clamping p into [L, U] gives the best count within the limits. A group with L > U raises the solver's infeasible error.

Cost. One read of the source and one sort per group, in a window that holds every row. Over 800,000 rows, with each supplier keeping its two offers with the most stock (measured 2026-11-12), the plan takes 1.05 times the CPU of the hand-written plan, and 0.044 s against Gurobi's 3.046 s, 70 times faster. A limit read from the data, such as the best tenth of all rows, adds a held-rows stage that checks every group before the first row leaves: 1.72 times the hand plan's CPU, and 14.5 times faster than Gurobi.

In this example each genre gets one or two films, and only films rated above 8 improve the objective:

SELECT title, genre, rating, pick FROM Films DECIDE pick(BOOL) SUCH THAT SUM(pick) by (genre) >= 1 AND SUM(pick) by (genre) <= 2 MAXIMIZE SUM((rating - 8) * pick) ORDER BY genre, rating DESC;

Drama and Sci-fi each have two films rated above 8, so p = 2 and both are chosen. Comedy has none, so p = 0 and the lower limit sets k = 1: its best film, Second Helping at exactly 8, is chosen at no cost to the objective.

S2: the best rows under nested quotas

S2 answers "the best rows under nested quotas": upper count limits whose groups nest, such as teams inside divisions inside a company-wide total.

A family of sets is laminar when any two of its sets are either disjoint or one contains the other. Upper quotas over a laminar family form a laminar matroid, where a greedy choice is still exact if it runs level by level, finest first.

The shape. A query is S2 when it has:

  • one BOOL per row and a linear score, as S1 reads them;
  • two or more upper count limits (SUM(x) <= k or < k) on different scopes, where a scope is a clause's by key, when and per. Clauses with the same scope form one level, whose cap is the least of theirs;
  • levels that form a chain: each coarser level's by key is a subset of the finer one's, compared as sets, with no when or the same one;
  • caps that are constants below 1e20, values of the row, or aggregates of the group.

The nesting is proved from the query's text; the data is never consulted. by (department) beside by (division) declines even when every department lies in one division, because the rule is chosen once, when the plan is built, and the IR holds no functional dependencies.

The algorithm.

  1. Finest level. Rank each group's rows by score and keep those ranked within the group's remaining cap (its cap less its rows fixed to 1).
  2. Each coarser level. Rank, in its own groups, only the rows the level before it keeps (ROW_NUMBER partitioned by the group and the previous level's keep flag), and keep those within its cap.
  3. Choose. A row is chosen when the last level keeps it and its score improves the objective.

Why it is exact. Within a finest group of cap q, only its q best positive rows can ever be useful. Once a quota's children are processed, any feasible selection can be swapped for one at least as good that uses only their survivors, so the parent loses nothing by ranking only the survivors. Each stage only removes rows, so a quota met below stays met. Ranking each level over all of its rows would be wrong: a division of cap 2 holding scores 9, 8 and 7, where 9 and 8 share a department of cap 1, must choose 9 and 7.

Cost. One sort per level. Over TPC-H customer at 150,000 rows, with at most 2 per nation and market segment, 5 per nation and 100 overall (measured 2026-11-12), the plan takes 1.41 times the CPU of a hand-written chain of ROW_NUMBER stages, and 0.052 s against Gurobi's 0.425 s. The linear relaxation of laminar quotas is integral, so Gurobi solves at its root node; its cost is building the model, which grows with the rows.

SELECT name, division, team, score, hire FROM Candidates DECIDE hire(BOOL) SUCH THAT SUM(hire) by (division, team) <= 1 AND SUM(hire) by (division) <= 2 AND SUM(hire) <= 4 MAXIMIZE SUM(score * hire);

The team level drops Bo, Fa and Io, the second candidates of their teams. The division level keeps Ada and Jo in Product, above Cy. The company level keeps the four best survivors, Ed, Ada, Jo and Hu, and drops Gu.

S3: as many rows as fit one budget

S3 answers "as many rows as fit one budget": every chosen row is worth the same, rows differ in weight, and one budget limits the total weight. Its mirror, "the fewest rows that reach a minimum", is S3 too.

The class is the maximum-cardinality knapsack. A knapsack whose rows have different values is the general 0-1 knapsack, which has no greedy answer and stays on the solver.

The shape. A query is S3 when it has:

  • one BOOL per row;
  • exactly one aggregate clause, SUM(w*x) <= B with a maximized count, or SUM(w*x) >= B with a minimized count, per by group or over all rows;
  • weights of any sign, zero included. Constant weights that add up to exactly 1 or -1 make the body a count, which is S1's, so S3 declines it and the two rules stay disjoint;
  • a limit that is a constant below 1e20, a value of the row (the group's tightest), or an aggregate of the group;
  • an objective that is a positive constant times the count, and other clauses only to fix single rows.

The algorithm. For each group:

  1. Sort. Order the free rows by weight, lightest first (heaviest first for a minimum).
  2. Running total. One window computes the running total of the weights and each row's rank. Both share one partition and one order, so DuckDB sorts once and a row's rank is the position at which its total is summed; two sorts could order tied rows differently.
  3. Size. A second window finds k: the largest rank whose total, plus the total of the rows fixed to 1, fits B (for a minimum, the smallest rank that reaches it). The empty prefix counts.
  4. Choose. Set x = 1 on the rows ranked within k.

Why it is exact. Among all sets of k rows, the k lightest have the smallest total, so some k rows fit exactly when the k lightest do. The longest prefix that fits is therefore the largest count. With negative weights the running totals fall before they rise, so the plan takes the largest rank that fits rather than stopping at the first that does not.

Cost. One sort per group and two windows, over one read. With a budget of 5,000 per supplier (measured 2026-11-12), the plan takes 1.53 times the CPU of a hand-written plan that cuts the running total at the first row over budget. That cut is correct for TPC-H's positive costs only; the rule pays for the largest fitting rank, which negative weights need. Against Gurobi at 800,000 rows: 0.094 s against 4.770 s (50.5 times) with a budget per supplier, and 0.207 s against 22.978 s (110.8 times) with one budget over all rows, in a fourth to a sixth of Gurobi's peak memory.

SELECT ticket, points, take FROM Backlog DECIDE take(BOOL) SUCH THAT SUM(points * take) <= 20 MAXIMIZE SUM(take) ORDER BY points;

The running totals of the sorted points are 1, 3, 6, 9, 14 and 19. The next ticket brings the total to 27, so the six smallest tickets are the answer.

A1: each row on its own

A1 answers problems in which no clause or objective term links two rows: each decision is optimized alone, inside its own range.

The class is the separable box-constrained problem: separable because the objective is a sum of terms that each read one decision, and box-constrained because each decision has only its own lower and upper bound.

The shape. A query is A1 when it has:

  • one or more BOOL, INT or REAL decisions per row;
  • only per-row bounds: a decision compared with a constant, a column or a deterministic expression of the row, with a coefficient, an offset and a when allowed (2*x + 1 <= cap). No aggregate clause;
  • an objective of linear terms and at most one square k*(w*x + t)^2 per decision, with k a nonzero constant, or no objective.

The algorithm. A chain of projections, with no sort, window or join:

  1. Read. Each bound clause becomes a lower and an upper end on its row, and the objective becomes a*x^2 + b*x per decision.
  2. Box. The row's box is the GREATEST of the lower ends and the LEAST of the upper ends, within the decision's own box: [0, 1] for BOOL, and [0, ∞) for INT and REAL unless a constant lower bound replaces the 0. An INT or BOOL box is rounded inward.
  3. Answer. Where the objective, as a minimization, is convex, the turning point -b / (2a) clamped into the box; otherwise the better end. For a whole number, the first integer whose next step no longer improves.
  4. Verify, then stream. A first read checks every row (an empty box is infeasible; an improving direction with no finite end is unbounded) into a one-row verdict, and a second read releases each row as it is computed (every row is read).

Why it is exact. The objective is a sum of independent terms, and every clause touches one decision, so improving one decision cannot change another's options or contribution. The best value of each decision together is a global optimum. A linear or quadratic function of one variable on an interval has its optimum at an end or at the turning point.

Cost. Two reads and no held rows. Shipping each of 800,000 offers as close to 5,000 units as its stock allows (measured 2026-11-09) takes 1.14 times the CPU of a hand-written plan that also reads the table twice, and 0.028 s against Gurobi's 0.353 s, 12.5 times faster, in a sixteenth of its peak memory.

Here each order's profit is margin * qty - 0.125 * qty^2, which peaks at 4 times the margin:

SELECT sku, margin, min_qty, max_qty, qty FROM Orders DECIDE qty(INT) SUCH THAT qty BETWEEN min_qty AND max_qty MAXIMIZE SUM(margin * qty - 0.125 * POWER(qty, 2));

A-100 peaks at 16 and C-300 at 10, both inside their ranges. B-200 and D-400 have negative margins, so their peaks lie below their ranges and each takes its lower end.

A2: one shared value closest to every row

A2 answers problems where many rows share one REAL decision, for the whole query or per key, and the objective measures its distance to a value on each row.

The class is single-facility location in one dimension: place one point to be as close as possible to many points on a line.

The shape. A query is A2 when it has:

  • one REAL decision, query-wide (per ():) or keyed (per K:; see the Guide);
  • bounds that reduce to one interval per class: the largest lower end and the smallest upper end over the class's rows;
  • one minimized loss of w*c + t, where the slope w is a nonzero constant: squared (SUM(k * POWER(...)), any weight k), absolute (SUM(k * ABS(...)), k a positive constant) or maximum absolute (MAX(k * ABS(...))).

The algorithm. Group then join: one hash aggregate per class computes the loss's statistics, and the class's answer, on one row, joins back to its rows on the class key.

LossAnswer before clamping
squaredthe weighted mean -b / (2a), from the sums a and b of the expanded squares; an end of the interval when the total weight a is 0 or less
absolutea median: the point nearest 0 between the lower and the upper median (quantile_disc)
maximum absolutethe midpoint of the least and the greatest value

The answer is then clamped into the class's interval. Each loss is a Strategy (SquareLoss, AbsoluteLoss, MaxAbsoluteLoss) that fills in its columns at each stage of one plan-builder skeleton, a Template Method.

Why it is exact. The mean minimizes the squared distance, a median the absolute distance, and the midpoint of the extremes the largest distance. With a positive total weight each loss is a convex function of one variable, so its minimum on an interval is its unconstrained minimum clamped into the interval. A squared loss whose total weight is 0 or less is linear or concave, and its minimum lies at an end. Where several points are optimal, A2 returns the one nearest 0.

Cost. One hash aggregate and one join, on every thread, and no window. With one squared loss over 800,000 rows (measured 2026-11-09), the plan takes 1.16 times the CPU of a hand-written plan, and 0.026 s against Gurobi's 0.077 s. The absolute loss is where the gap opens: the solver path adds a helper decision per row for each ABS, and keyed it takes 12.322 s against 0.039 s rewritten, 313 times faster, in a fortieth of Gurobi's peak memory.

SELECT highway, store, mile, depot FROM Stores DECIDE per highway: depot(REAL) SUCH THAT depot <= 100 MINIMIZE SUM(POWER(depot - mile, 2));

Each depot sits at its highway's mean mile: (3 + 8 + 9 + 20) / 4 = 10 on I-10, and (2 + 6 + 40) / 3 = 16 on I-95. The store at mile 40 pulls the I-95 depot to 16. With the absolute distance of the depot example, the depot moves to the median, mile 6.

R1: fill a budget by value per unit

R1 answers problems where REAL amounts, each with its own range, share one budget. The best plan fills the budget in order of value per unit of budget.

The class is the continuous (fractional) knapsack: a knapsack whose items can be taken in part.

The shape. A query is R1 when it has:

  • one or more REAL decisions per row, with any per-row bounds; a bound of magnitude 1e20 or more is no bound, as on the solver;
  • one budget, L <= SUM(w*x) <= U, written with <=, >=, = or BETWEEN, per by group or over all rows. The weight w is read per row and may have any sign or be zero. The limits may be constants, columns or aggregates;
  • a linear objective, or none.

The algorithm. Each item has a break-even price t = v / w, its value per unit of budget. At a price per unit of budget, an item whose t is above the price belongs at the end of its range that spends more, and one below it at the end that spends less. Spending falls as the price rises, so one sort by t finds the price at which spending meets the budget. Items tied at that price share what is left in proportion to their room, so the answer is the same on every run. The plan has five stages:

  1. Items. Each row's values: its ends, weight, value, price and room.
  2. Group totals. A hash aggregate per group: the spend at a zero price, the room in each direction, and the verdicts (infeasible, unbounded).
  3. Price. One window ordered by t over the narrow items, then an aggregate that picks the group's price.
  4. Sums at the price. A hash aggregate of what the items above, below and at the price spend.
  5. Answer. Each row joins its group's numbers and computes its amount.

Why it is exact. A price certifies the answer, as a Lagrange multiplier does. Take a price λ and an assignment x within the budget in which every item sits at the end its net value v - λw prefers, where λ > 0 only if the budget is spent to its upper limit and λ < 0 only if it is spent to its lower limit. For any feasible y, Σv·y = Σ(v − λw)y + λΣw·y ≤ Σ(v − λw)x + λΣw·x = Σv·x. The plan builds exactly such an x at the price it finds. The argument uses no sign of a weight and no order between items.

Cost. One sort of the narrow items, several hash aggregates and joins. With one budget over 800,000 rows (measured 2026-11-09), the plan takes 0.96 times the CPU of a hand-written plan. With a budget per part it takes 1.93 times, because each group's checks must raise before any row is released, even under LIMIT, so the plan aggregates every row of each group and joins the verdict back. Against Gurobi it at 800,000 rows: 0.092 s against 1.132 s with one budget, and 0.120 s against 1.386 s with a budget per part, 11.5 to 12.3 times faster.

SELECT good, value_per_kg, available_kg, ROUND(kg, 1) AS kg FROM Cargo DECIDE kg(REAL) SUCH THAT kg <= available_kg AND SUM(kg) <= 1000 MAXIMIZE SUM(value_per_kg * kg);

Saffron, coffee and tea come first by value per kg and are taken whole, 502 kg. Rice is the item at the price: it fills the remaining 498 kg. Sugar, below the price, stays at 0.

R4: a fair split of one budget

R4 answers MAXIMIZE MIN(c * x) under one budget and a cap per row: raise the smallest normalized share as high as the budget and the caps allow.

The class is weighted max-min fair allocation. In MIN(x / need) the coefficient c is 1 / need, so the objective is the smallest share of need served.

The shape. A query is R4 when it has:

  • one REAL decision, per row or per key;
  • the objective MAXIMIZE MIN(c * x): one plain MIN over one term whose coefficient is read from the row (see the worst case in the Guide);
  • one budget, SUM(w*x) <= B, the only aggregate clause, with a weight of any sign, per by group or over all rows;
  • a box from 0 to a cap: the lower bound is 0, and every other clause is an upper bound on the row.

The algorithm. Every row the MIN reads must reach a common level s, so x >= s / c. Two things bound s. The budget allows s ≤ B / N, where N is the sum of w / c over the rows with a positive weight. Each cap allows s ≤ cap * c. The level is the smaller bound, and a row with a positive weight takes exactly s / c. A row with a negative weight takes its cap, which frees budget for the others. One hash aggregate per budget group computes the sums, and a second read joins each row to its group's level.

Why it is exact. Any assignment whose smallest share is s spends at least s·N of the budget and must respect s ≤ cap * c on every row. The plan takes the highest level both bounds allow and gives every row exactly that level, which meets the budget and every cap. Leftover budget stays unused, since the objective has no preference for where it goes.

Cost. One hash aggregate and one join, no sort. Over 800,000 rows of partsupp with the weight read from ps_supplycost, the plan takes 1.03 times the CPU of a hand-written plan and answers in 0.026 s. Gurobi takes 2,158 s on the same query: it is fast while a cap sets the level and slow once the budget binds, because the max-min objective becomes one helper variable bounded by every row.

SELECT program, need, max_alloc, ROUND(alloc, 2) AS alloc, ROUND(alloc / need, 3) AS share FROM Programs DECIDE alloc(REAL) SUCH THAT alloc <= max_alloc AND SUM(alloc) <= 100 MAXIMIZE MIN(alloc / need);

The needs add up to 110, so the budget alone would allow a level of 100 / 110 = 0.909. Shelter's cap allows only 25 / 30 = 0.833, which is smaller, so every program gets 83.3% of its need and 8.33 units stay unspent.

N1: stay inside one sum of squares

N1 answers problems with one limit SUM(POWER(x, 2)) <= B on REAL decisions: the feasible set is a ball around zero, or an ellipsoid when each row weighs its square.

The class is Euclidean-ball projection (the nearest point of a ball to a target) and the support function of an ellipsoid (the best point of it for a linear objective).

The shape. A query is N1 when it has:

  • one REAL decision per row;
  • exactly one aggregate clause, SUM(c * POWER(s * x, 2)) <= B, with c a positive constant and s a constant or a value of the row. POWER(x, 2), x * x and norm(x, 2) all read this way;
  • a box of exactly [0, ∞) or (−∞, ∞). Any other bound declines, even one that would not bind, because the answer is then the projection onto a clipped ball, which one scale does not give;
  • a linear objective, one convex square per row with its linear terms (a squared distance to a target), or no objective. A slope that differs between rows admits only a linear objective.

The algorithm. With a target y (from the square), the answer is y when it lies inside the ball, and otherwise y scaled toward zero to the ball's surface. With a linear objective of direction d, the answer is d scaled to the surface. On [0, ∞), negative parts of y or d become 0 first. One hash aggregate per ball computes the squared length h, its square root is taken once per ball, and every row joins its ball's scale.

Why it is exact. The nearest point of a ball to an outside target lies on the segment from the center to the target, so projecting is scaling. For a linear objective, d·x ≤ ‖d‖·‖x‖ ≤ ‖d‖·√B by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with equality when x points along d and reaches the surface. A weight per row becomes a ball again after substituting z = sqrt(q) * x. On [0, ∞), setting a coordinate with a negative target to 0 keeps the point feasible and brings it no farther from the target.

Cost. One hash aggregate and one join. Over 800,000 rows (measured 2026-11-09), the plan takes 0.032 s and 40 MiB at its peak against Gurobi's 3.295 s and 727 MiB, 102 times faster; against a hand-written plan it takes 1.29 times the CPU. N1 also answers on a machine without a Gurobi license, where the solver path refuses a quadratic limit.

SELECT name, score, ROUND(x, 3) AS x FROM Signals DECIDE x(REAL) SUCH THAT SUM(POWER(x, 2)) <= 4 MINIMIZE SUM(POWER(x - score, 2));

The target (3, 4, 0) has length 5, outside the ball of radius 2, so it is scaled by 2 / 5 to (1.2, 1.6, 0).

O1: match two tables by position

O1 answers one-to-one matchings: a BOOL for every pair of a cross product, each row of each side matched exactly once, and a cost that is a distance between positions or a product.

The class is the Monge assignment. A cost is Monge when uncrossing two pairs never raises it; for such a cost the matching in sorted order is optimal, with no search.

The shape. A query is O1 when it has:

  • a source that is a cross product of two tables; a WHERE on one side is allowed, since filter pushdown places it inside that side;
  • one BOOL per pair, and exactly two clauses, per K: SUM(x) by (K) = 1, one per side, with no when (see per in the Guide);
  • one cost: ABS(a - b) or POWER(a - b, 2), minimized; or a * b, maximized or minimized;
  • positions declared NOT NULL on their tables, and a key that determines the position: the whole row (per d) or a key column of its table.

The algorithm.

  1. Verify. Check the cost's coefficient on every pair into a one-row verdict.
  2. Rank. Rank each side by position, with a window on that side's own rows, below the cross product. This is eager aggregation (Yan and Larson) applied to windows: a step whose keys come from one side runs on that side's n rows, not on the pairs.
  3. Pair. Over the cross product, set x = 1 where the two ranks are equal (opposite, for a minimized product), and raise the solver's infeasible error when the two sides, both nonempty, hold different numbers of classes.

Why it is exact. For positions a ≤ a′ and b ≤ b′, pairing in order costs no more than crossing the pairs, for both the absolute and the squared distance. Any crossed matching can be uncrossed one pair at a time without getting worse, which ends at the sorted matching. For a product, the rearrangement inequality gives equal ranks for a maximum and opposite ranks for a minimum. The positions must be declared NOT NULL: SUM drops a NULL term, so a NULL position would make its row free, and ranking would no longer place it. A declared constraint holds for every row; statistics would not prove it.

Cost. One sort of each side's n rows, while the solver builds one binary per pair, n² in all. With 1,600 suppliers a side, 2,560,000 pairs (measured 2026-11-10), the plan takes 0.057 s and 30 MiB against Gurobi's 11.732 s and 5,060 MiB, 206 times faster. Against a hand-written plan at 10,240,000 pairs it takes 2.87 times the CPU, for the check of every pair's coefficient and the class ranks the rule needs for duplicate rows.

SELECT driver, pickup, driver_km, pickup_km FROM ( SELECT d.driver, p.pickup, d.km AS driver_km, p.km AS pickup_km, x FROM Drivers d CROSS JOIN Pickups p DECIDE x(BOOL) SUCH THAT per d: SUM(x) by (d) = 1 AND per p: SUM(x) by (p) = 1 MINIMIZE SUM(ABS(d.km - p.km) * x) ) WHERE x = 1 ORDER BY driver;
